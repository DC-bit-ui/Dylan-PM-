## 2. The de-risking layer and the counterfactual

**Dylan:** From the persona of a bank, a lot of these metrics are complex and technical —

Then show the modelled projection of what would have happened if the manager *hadn't* changed practices. And expand the area of interest to the neighbours: this property in a carbon project versus the neighbour over the fence under conventional management. ## 4. Cow-as-hero — the framing rule

**Dylan:** Riffing off villain/hero: we're also transforming perception. The central point is the **grazing land management method** — that's how we produce the credits. To date, cows have been cast as the ecological villains of climate change (greenhouse-gas accounting, the veganism movement). But cows are the *mechanism* by which we achieve ecological restoration and prevention. Better grazing land management means a healthy agricultural system that leads to ecological restoration, better water quality, better reef outcomes, better climate outcomes — and a more positive perception of our industry.

**Hobbs:** We have to spin the story that having cows on the land creates *healthier* land than if it were left wild — using them as a tool to optimise the land. Otherwise it's too easy for people to say "just do away with the farms," which lets them keep calling the cow the villain. So we must not focus on beef at all — no language around beef production — because we have to actively shift the paradigm to **land stewardship**. We can't cede moral high ground to the vegan/anti-cow movement. The frame has to be: we'll have healthier land than has ever existed in Australia if we keep doing this right. It's not an indulgence; it's a core necessity for landscape function. By reducing randomness and managing in a planned way, we create better outcomes than random nature would. We can't make the cow "less bad" — we have to make the cow the straight-up hero. The cow needs a cape; the cow and the farmer together, like Superman and Superdog.
